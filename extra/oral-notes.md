# Five-minute oral script

Five-minute script organized by slide. The welfare claim is a hypothesis to evaluate. The final slide includes the student's handwritten derivation photo.

**0:00–0:20 — Title.** This is Acemoglu, Kong and Ozdaglar’s paper on AI, human cognition and knowledge collapse. My question is why better advice for an individual might weaken the knowledge that everybody relies on. The repository link contains the source, computations and the longer explanation.

**0:20–1:20 — The paper and the agent.** An agent needs to predict a general state and a state specific to their own situation. Gaussian information lets us summarize what they know by two precisions. Public precision X is inherited. Individual precision Y adds prior information, human effort and the AI signal. The person chooses effort to maximize expected task output minus its cost. Human effort also creates a thin public signal for future cohorts, but the short-lived, atomistic person does not internalize that contribution. The crucial production assumption is that context-specific information has no standalone value without general knowledge. That is Delta I equals zero.

**1:20–2:35 — Main result and conditions.** The derivative of the Gaussian success probability is positive but decreasing in precision. Differentiate the payoff first with respect to effort. Increasing X raises that marginal return through the complementarity term. Increasing agentic accuracy lowers it, because AI already supplies the kind of precision that effort would have produced. These strict signs require positive public precision and the listed Gaussian and production assumptions. Strict concavity then gives a unique interior optimum and turns the cross-partial signs into effort responses. At zero public knowledge, the baseline chooses zero effort. None of this says that lower effort must lower welfare: at fixed X, optimized utility actually increases with AI accuracy. Long-run knowledge changes too. The paper’s stronger single-peak welfare statements have an additional prior-precision restriction, and even then the optimal AI accuracy can be zero.

**2:35–3:35 — What I did.** I used Lean to check ten static proof endpoints, with no proof holes or added axioms. Starting from the Gaussian integral, the formalization derives the derivatives, first-order condition, payoff curvature and both cross-partials. I then restored a positive standalone return to context-specific information, which Section 5 does not do. Both cross-partial signs survive. At zero public knowledge, Lean also proves that some positive effort gives higher utility than zero effort. The substitution mechanism survives, while the exact zero-effort boundary depends on zero standalone value. These are static proofs: the paper's sixteen numbered propositions on dynamics, welfare and extensions remain unformalized.

**3:35–5:00 — Where I challenged the reasoning.** [Show the handwritten photo.] The photo shows my Gaussian derivatives, baseline first-order condition and cross-partials. The extension's boundary calculation appears alongside it on the slide and is checked in Lean. The tempting extrapolation is that better AI must increase welfare. My verdict is narrower: yes at fixed public knowledge, but not necessarily in the long run. I checked the authors’ own extensions. They change aggregation, synthetic information and how effort creates public knowledge. They retain the assumption that context-specific knowledge alone is worthless. My extension preserves crowd-out but removes the reason for choosing exactly zero effort at zero inherited knowledge. That is not a new theorem saying AI always helps; it identifies which part of the collapse story is robust and which part needs the essential-input assumption.

## Short answers for questions

- **Why not put the current public return in the FOC?** An individual is atomistic and short-lived; their infinitesimal contribution benefits future cohorts and is not privately internalized.
- **Why posterior means under a tolerance payoff?** For a symmetric unimodal Normal posterior, a fixed-width interval captures the most probability when centered at its mean.
- **Is Delta I equals zero needed for the two signs?** No. Positive Delta X and diminishing returns to individual precision sustain those signs even with positive Delta I. Essentiality is central to the zero-effort boundary.
- **Does reduced effort prove welfare falls?** No. Lower effort saves costs. The static envelope theorem removes the first-order effort adjustment. Long-run welfare also contains the loss of inherited public knowledge.
- **Is the optimal precision strictly positive?** Not guaranteed. The propositions allow zero. Saying “interior” without qualification overstates them.
- **What is the Bayesian link?** Signals add precision; independent state drift adds variance. The static optimal effort enters the next cohort’s public information update.
- **Did you reproduce collapse thresholds?** No. The assignment treats dynamics as read-only; the code only checks static incentives.
- **Does Lean verify the whole paper?** No. Ten endpoints cover selected static results and the original standalone-value extension. Observation 1 is formalized; the effort-response result is conditional on the differentiated FOC. Optimizer existence, the Bayesian foundation, dynamic equilibria and Propositions 1–16 still need proofs. The complete coverage audit is in `lean/audit/full-paper-coverage.md`.
- **Does Section 5 preserve literal collapse?** Not in the synthetic-data extension with positive exogenous public precision: its lowest steady state is positive. The authors retain crowd-out, not every literal baseline conclusion.
- **What did you not settle?** The critical equality cases, the full dynamics under positive Delta I, empirical calibration, and an unrestricted optimal policy.
